Your Brain on Drugs

Why drugs feel powerful, why they are hard to quit, and why it is never "just willpower".

The reward system

Your brain has a reward system that makes you feel good when you do things that help you survive, like eating, laughing with friends or achieving a goal. It works by releasing a chemical called dopamine.

Drugs can trigger a much bigger flood of dopamine than normal activities. The brain learns: "This is important. Do it again." That is why a drug can start to feel like a need instead of a choice.

From trying to dependence

  1. ExperimentationUsually driven by curiosity, peer pressure or stress. Even one time carries risks.
  2. Regular useThe drug becomes part of a routine. Mood, school and friendships can start to change.
  3. ToleranceThe brain adapts, so the same amount has less effect. People may take more, which raises the danger.
  4. DependenceThe body and brain feel they need the drug to feel normal. Stopping brings withdrawal and strong cravings.

Not everyone moves through all stages, and nobody can predict who will become dependent. That uncertainty is part of the risk.

Why teens are more vulnerable

  • The brain keeps developing until around the mid-20s, especially the areas for planning, self-control and decision-making.
  • Drugs can interfere with learning, memory and mood while these skills are still being built.
  • Starting early is linked to a higher chance of developing a problem later on.
Addiction is a health condition It is not a character flaw or a lack of willpower. It changes how the brain works, and like other chronic conditions, it can be treated. Many people recover with support from health professionals, family and friends.

Why do people start?

Curiosity

Wanting to know what it feels like, or seeing it in movies, music or online.

Peer pressure

Wanting to fit in, or fearing being left out or teased.

Stress & feelings

Trying to escape pressure, sadness, anxiety or problems at home or school.

What protects you

  • Strong, supportive relationships with family, friends and teachers.
  • Healthy ways to handle stress: sports, music, hobbies, talking things out.
  • Knowing the facts and having a plan for how you will say no.
  • Feeling that you belong, such as in a club, team, church group or org.
  • Asking for help early, long before things feel out of control.
